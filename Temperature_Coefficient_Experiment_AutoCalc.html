To determine the temperature coefficient of resistance of the material of a wire.

6.5 Theory

The temperature coefficient of resistance is the property of the material of a substance. It is the measure of change in electrical resistance of a substance per degree temperature change.

For a conductor, the resistance increases linearly and for a semiconductor, the resistance decreases exponentially with temperature.

For a conductor, the resistance increases with an increase in temperature. If \(R_1\) is the resistance of a conductor at temperature \(\theta_1\) and at a higher temperature \(\theta_2\) the resistance rises to \(R_2\), then we can write:

\( R_2 = R_1 [1 + \alpha(\theta_2 - \theta_1)] \)     (1)

where \(\alpha\) is the temperature coefficient of the material of the conductor.
Rearranging Eq. 1, we get:

\( R_2 = R_1 + \alpha R_1 (\theta_2 - \theta_1) \)
\( \alpha R_1 (\theta_2 - \theta_1) = R_2 - R_1 \)
\( \alpha = \dfrac{R_2 - R_1}{R_1(\theta_2 - \theta_1)} = \dfrac{\Delta R}{R_1 \Delta\theta} \)     (2)

where \(\Delta R (= R_2 - R_1)\) is the change in resistance due to the change in temperature \(\Delta\theta (= \theta_2 - \theta_1)\).

In Eq. (2) if we put \(R_1 = 1 \Omega\) and \(\Delta\theta = 1 ^\circ C\) we get \(\alpha = \Delta R\), thus we define the temperature coefficient of resistance of a substance as the change in resistance per unit resistance (per ohm) for unit change in temperature (per \(^\circ C\)). Also, we find the unit of \(\alpha\) as per \(^\circ C^{-1}\).

For a conductor, if we know the resistances \(R_1\) at a temperature \(\theta_1\) and \(R_2\) at a higher temperature \(\theta_2\), then we can calculate the temperature coefficient of resistance of its material by using Eq. 2. The unknown resistance can be determined using a voltmeter and an ammeter employing Ohm’s law.

Ohm's law states that at a constant temperature, the current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across those two points. Introducing the constant of proportionality, the resistance, one arrives at the usual mathematical equation that describes this relationship:

\( V = IR \)

where \(I\) is the current and \(V\) is the potential difference across the resistance \(R\).

6.6 Equipment

Power supply, voltmeter, ammeter, coil of conducting wire, commutator, thermometer, Rheostat (variable resistor), Beaker, water, electric heater, etc.

6.8 Procedure

6.8.1 Set up and Data collection.

  1. Set up the apparatus as the circuit diagram shows in figure 6.3.
  2. Fill the beaker so that the resistance coil is immersed in the water.
  3. Using both the voltage control knob of the DC source and the rheostat \(R_h\) choose a current of 0.1 A. Switch off the circuit immediately and wait for at least 2 minutes. Note down the temperature \(\theta_1'\)
  4. Switch on the circuit and note down the current from the ammeter and voltage from the voltmeter. Switch off the circuit and wait for 1 minute.
  5. Now adjust the current to 0.2 A following step 3 and note down both ammeter and voltmeter readings as before the temperature. Switch off the circuit and wait for 1 minute.
  6. Repeat previous step for 0.3A, 0.4A, & 0.5A current.
  7. Note down the temperature \(\theta_1''\) of the system again. Mean of \(\theta_1'\) and \(\theta_1''\) is the initial temperature of the coil.
  8. Pour hot water in the Beaker to raise the temperature. Note the high temperature, \(\theta_2'\) from the thermometer.
  9. Repeat steps 3 to 6 and note down the temperature of the beaker from the thermometer \(\theta_2''\) again. Average of \(\theta_2'\) and \(\theta_2''\) gives the high temperature of the beaker.

6.8.2 Data analysis

  1. Use EXCEL to plot V versus I graphs on the same graph for room temperature (\(\theta_1\)) and high temperature (\(\theta_2\)) data.
  2. Find the best fit lines (trend line in Excel) and determine the slope of the best fit lines from the respective trendline equations.
  3. The slope of the V vs I graphs gives the resistance values \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) respectively. Calculate the value of \(\alpha\) using equation 2.
  4. Compare your result with the reference value of \(\alpha\) for copper material.
